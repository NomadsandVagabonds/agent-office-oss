## Design Context

### Users
People coordinating agents in The Office, taking a short break via the elevator. The owner wants cultural office spoofs and small games: recognizable places to explore without leaving the building's voxel world or disturbing ongoing work.

### Brand Personality
Affectionate, mundane, absurd. Reward exploration, small observations, and harmless office mischief. Each floor may have its own mood, from sitcom warmth to quiet workplace unease, while keeping the humor grounded in ordinary office objects.

### Aesthetic Direction
Use the house's chunky voxel scale and actual Vox geometry across distinct cultural break floors. Nothing is addressed to the player: feedback lives in the room and on its equipment, and the only words are ones the office itself would have up, per the level kit. Original models, layouts, and workplace jokes establish each homage without borrowed game or television assets.

- **Scranton Paper Co.**: a small Pennsylvania paper-company branch with beige carpet, wood veneer, venetian blinds, fluorescent ceiling panels, reception, sales islands, a manager's office, a conference room, and a kitchenette. First-person exploration and an optional timed prank.
- **Night Shift**: a monochrome office stealth game inspired by the atmosphere of Limbo. A side-on orthographic camera, black voxel silhouettes, distant windows, dust, and sweeping white security beams replace the forest setting. Light exposes the player; low desks and darkness provide cover. Keep the scene legible enough to learn by watching. The one colour on the floor is the long game's coin, in the house gold, so that it is known for what it is; the keycard the floor hides is the house's card in the floor's own greys.

### Design Principles
1. This is a break from coordination; local play never changes real work.
2. Make the room recognizable through layout, props, and original workplace jokes.
3. Keep stakes forgiving: Scranton's timer is optional; Night Shift has unlimited checkpoint retries and a pause key.
4. Show consequences in the room: a stapler disappears, gelatin wobbles, a beam stops at cover, a shutter opens when power is cut.
5. Keep the shared elevator and Escape return-to-work control. Adapt movement to each game and explain none of it: no control legends, instruction cards, objective banners or captions on any floor. Words exist only as things an office really has on its walls and desks (a door plaque, a memo that reads like a memo, a wall clock, a device's own readout), and the one prompt is the level kit's `E <name>` chip. If a mechanic cannot be found without words, make the room show it (a light that goes dark, a lever that rattles, an owner who objects) rather than tell the player. Being a little lost is part of the point; the controls are written in `docs/`, for developers.
6. Pause active game time when the visitor is interrupted. Respect reduced-motion settings without removing necessary gameplay signals.
