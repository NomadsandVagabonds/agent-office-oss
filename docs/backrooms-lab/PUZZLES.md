# Out of Office: twelve puzzle rooms

A proposal. Nothing here is built. Each room has one rule that nobody states,
an answer the seed moves, and a door you can walk out of without solving
anything. None has a key, a code, a fuse or a card. The owner picks; rooms are
then built one at a time on the room contract (`docs/BACKROOMS.md`, "Adding a
room").

This file is generated from the same data as the page the owner reads, so the
two say the same thing.

## What makes a puzzle a joy

Principles only. No puzzle, level or look is taken from anywhere. Where a
source could not be opened, the entry says so.

1. **Ask why it is here.** Every element should answer "why is this here?" inside the room's own fiction. "a designer should look at each element of the player experience of an escape room and ask "Why is this here?"" — [Scott Nicholson, "Ask Why: Creating a Better Player Experience Through Environmental Storytelling and Consistency in Escape Room Design", Meaningful Play 2016](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/askwhy.pdf)
2. **Beyond codes into locks.** Codes into padlocks and counting things in pictures are what the genre falls back on. A puzzle should move the story on, delight, or give an aha. "an adventure that goes beyond a series of paper-based puzzles, counting items in pictures on the walls, entering codes on padlocks" — [Scott Nicholson, "Peeking Behind the Locked Door: A Survey of Escape Room Facilities", 2015 (175 facilities)](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/erfacwhite.pdf) The survey's commonest puzzle type is searching for hidden objects (78% of rooms). It reports how common locks are and urges going beyond them; it does not use the word "overused".
3. **Know you are right before you try.** The player should be sure of the answer before committing to it. Never make them try every option. "the team should be confident that their answer is the correct answer before trying it in a lock." — [Nicholson 2016 (above); Errol Elumir, "13 Rules for Escape Room Puzzle Design", 2018, rule 4](https://thecodex.ca/13-rules-for-escape-room-puzzle-design/)
4. **No planted red herrings.** Players make their own red herrings out of decoration. Do not punish them for looking at what is interesting. "Escape rooms should not punish people for exploring interesting things in the gamespace." — [David Spira, "Red Herrings in Escape Rooms [Design]", Room Escape Artist, 2019; Nicholson 2016](https://roomescapeartist.com/2019/02/10/red-herrings/)
5. **The best thing in the room must matter.** The most striking object in a room has to have a purpose. "The coolest things in the room need to have a purpose." — [David Spira, "Chekhov's Gun & His Other Belongings in Escape Rooms", Room Escape Artist, 2022](https://roomescapeartist.com/2022/08/06/chekhovs-gun-escape-rooms)
6. **Clue everything; the link must make sense afterwards.** Nothing is guessed. The connection behind an aha has to feel obvious in hindsight. "Aha! Correlations Should Make Sense" — [Elumir 2018, rules 2 and 6](https://thecodex.ca/13-rules-for-escape-room-puzzle-design/)
7. **Keep the doing short.** Once the idea is had, carrying it out should take seconds. The worst outcome is finding out your time was wasted. "The worst outcome that can happen is learning you wasted your time." — [Elumir 2018](https://thecodex.ca/13-rules-for-escape-room-puzzle-design/) Elumir's limit is five minutes of process in a sixty-minute room. Scaling that to seconds for a five-minute battery is my inference.
8. **Answer every attempt.** Right or wrong, the room responds. Feedback is what lets people solve it themselves. "Giving them proper feedback cuts down your hinting and increases their satisfaction of solving the puzzle themselves." — [Elumir 2018, rule 10](https://thecodex.ca/13-rules-for-escape-room-puzzle-design/)
9. **The clock and the hints belong to the world.** A timer that makes no sense in the fiction is a missed chance. Let something in the world run down instead. "Instead of having a clock that doesn't make sense in the world, designers should consider other ways to indicate that time is passing" — [Nicholson 2016](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/askwhy.pdf)
10. **One idea at a time, and watch people play.** Teach a single idea, then split up anything testers stall on. "Watching them lets you monitor their moment-to-moment experience." — [Kim Swift and others, "Thinking With Portals: Creating Valve's New IP", Game Developer magazine, 2008](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/thinking-with-portals-creating-valve-s-new-ip)
11. **Introduce, develop, twist, conclude.** A mechanic is met safely, used again, then turned into something the player did not expect it to be. "something crazy happens that makes you think about it in a way you weren't expecting." — [Koichi Hayashida, interviewed by Christian Nutt, "The secret to Mario level design", Gamasutra, 2012](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-secret-to-i-mario-i-level-design)
12. **Simpler puzzle, stronger epiphany.** The less there is in a puzzle, the harder the realisation lands. Nothing should stand out unless it is meant to. "The clearer and simpler the puzzle is, the more beautiful and strong that feeling of epiphany can be." — [Jonathan Blow, quoted in Leigh Alexander, "The Witness: Modeling epiphany", Gamasutra, 2014](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-i-the-witness-i-modeling-epiphany)
13. **Never trial and error.** If the only way to the answer is luck or trying everything, it is a bad puzzle. Show the problem before the solution. "If the solution can only be reached by trial and error or plain luck, it's a bad puzzle." — [Ron Gilbert, "Why Adventure Games Suck", 1989](https://grumpygamer.com/why_adventure_games_suck)
14. **Gate by knowledge, so the rule is the reward.** **(not verified)** What opens the way is something understood, which the player keeps, and not something carried. — [Thinky Games, "Metroidbrainia: an in-depth exploration of knowledge-gated games"](https://thinkygames.com/features/metroidbrainia-an-in-depth-exploration-of-knowledge-gated-games/) Not verified. The page refused the request; only its search snippet was seen. Raph Koster's "A Theory of Fun" (fun as learning a pattern) supports the same point and is cited from memory.
15. **An aha is what people keep.** **(abstract only)** Solutions reached by insight are remembered better than ones ground out, so in a five-minute visit they are what a player takes home. — [Danek, Fraps, von Müller, Grothe and Öllinger, "Aha! experiences leave a mark: facilitated recall of insight solutions", Psychological Research 77(5), 2013](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00426-012-0454-8) Abstract only: 64.4% of insight solutions recalled after 14 days against 52.4% of the others. The full paper was not opened.
16. **Never colour alone, never sound alone.** Colour and sound back up another signal. Neither carries anything essential by itself. "An easy test is to ask someone to play through for the first time with the sound muted." — [Game Accessibility Guidelines, basic tier](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/ensure-no-essential-information-is-conveyed-by-sounds-alone/)

## What we have, as a player

I played seed 7 and stood in the rooms below with the dev page, and read every builder. As a player, the level already has one piece of real puzzle teaching: the cardboard people teach that things move unwatched, and the hallway that is not sure turns that into a room you operate by turning your back. That pair is the model for everything proposed here.

| Has the seed of a puzzle | Why |
|---|---|
| Status Codes (many-doors) | Already a knowledge gate with an answer the seed moves: the 200 door opens. Wrong doors only rattle. |
| Input Device (keyboard-floor) | The best toy in the deck. It answers fifteen commands and asks for none of them. |
| It's For You (phone) | Has a rule (it stops when you get near) and nothing on the other side of it. |
| The Standees, The Hall That Is Not Sure | Teach and twist. Leave alone. |
| Hydration Corridor (coolers) | A looking trick (the corridor only lengthens while you face the dark). Works as it is. |
| The five rooms with residents | Each has one readable rule and a cost in hearts. They are encounters more than puzzles, and that is the right amount of them. |

Set dressing, and fine as that: Seating Plan, Caution, Standing Meeting, Hanging Garden, The Collection, Team Photo, Breakout Rooms, The Party, Employee of the Month, Refreshments, The Stress Pool, The Board, Going Up. They are jokes you read. Most of the deck should stay this way; a floor where every oddity wanted solving would lose the emptiness.

| Best three | The one change |
|---|---|
| Status Codes | Let each wrong door answer as its own code. 404 opens on wallpaper. 403 rattles. 301 swings to show an arrow to another door. 503 opens the second time you try it. After three wrong doors, 429 locks them all for ten seconds. Failing becomes the funny part and tells you something (principle 8). |
| Input Device | Start the screen on LOGIN: with a waiting cursor. Typing your own name already gets YES. THAT IS YOU. Make that pay: one tube comes on over a battery pack. The answer is something every player knows and no two players share (principle 6). |
| It's For You | Let it be answered by somebody who gets to it without looking at it. Picked up mid-ring, the nearest tube toward the nearest battery pack flickers. The gag stays for everyone who walks straight at it. |

## The rooms, in the order I would build them

### 1. Now Serving (`now-serving`) — build first

*There is a queue of cardboard people and it only moves when you stop watching it. So you queue properly, with your back to the room.*

- **First ten seconds.** A serving hatch with its shutter down and a number over it: NOW SERVING 41. A line of standees between tape posts, facing the hatch. Painted footprints at the back of the line. You look round the room, as anybody does, and a bell goes: the number is 42 and there is one fewer of them. Step out of the footprints while your back is turned and one of them is standing in your place when you look again.
- **The aha.** Watching was what held the queue up. And your place is only yours while you are standing in it.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** How long the queue is (two to five), which of two or three hatches is the live one, whether the line bends, and what is served. So the number of times you must look away and where you must stand are never the same. Two parts down it stacks with the mascot, who also only moves unwatched: every look away that moves the queue is a move for him too, and you have to count.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: you lose your place, six to ten seconds a position. Solving: a battery pack and a snack on the counter. The hatch is on the back wall and the doors are left and right, so you can walk straight through.
- **Verbs.** Looking away. Standing in a place. Listening (the bell), backed by the number changing and the line shortening.
- **Why it is not busywork.** The only thing it asks you to do is stop looking, and in this building that is the one thing you have learned not to do.
- **What it needs.** Nothing from the conductor. Standees and their rule exist. New: a shutter, a two-digit display, a pure queue rule (watched, in place, dt). About 8 x 8 m, 8,000 triangles, 8 meshes, 6 lights. Needs: unwatched. Teaches: blink.
- **Stands on.** 11 (Introduce, develop, twist, conclude); 8 (Answer every attempt)

### 2. Room Booking (`room-booking`) — build first

*Three meeting rooms with booking panels. The time on your camcorder tells you which is empty. Get it wrong and you are in a meeting.*

- **First ten seconds.** A lobby and three identical doors. Beside each, a lit panel says AVAILABLE and lists the day's bookings in the same block digits as the stamp in the corner of your picture: PM 7:00 – PM 8:30, with a real project's name. Your picture says PM 7:22. Light moves under two of the doors and there is a murmur behind them. Under the third it is still.
- **The aha.** The date and time on the picture are not decoration. They are the time here, and the room's own AVAILABLE is the place talking.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** The stamp is already rolled for each part of the building, and the bookings are rolled round it, so the free room and the reason it is free change every visit. The clock runs: a room free now may be booked in forty seconds, and if you are still in it when the meeting starts, they are in the chairs when you look round. Two parts down: no light under the doors, panels in 24-hour time, and all three booked with one meeting about to end.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: you are walked to the empty chair and sat through eight seconds of ANY QUESTIONS? Eight seconds of battery, no hearts. Solving: what the last meeting left, a snack and a battery pack. Doors left and right, so the lobby is a way through.
- **Verbs.** The camcorder's date stamp. Looking under a door (crouching helps and is not needed). Listening. Shutting a door. The office's real projects and tasks.
- **Why it is not busywork.** It is one comparison, you know the answer before you touch the handle, and the penalty is a joke worth seeing once.
- **What it needs.** One thing from the lead: the running time of the stamp, read-only, on c.env (hud.js keeps it). The rest is in the room: doors of its own, three 3 x 3 m rooms inside its footprint, a seat taken with player.warp. About 10 x 9 m, 15,000 triangles, 12 meshes, 8 lights. Teaches: stamp.
- **Stands on.** 9 (The clock and the hints belong to the world); 3 (Know you are right before you try)

### 3. Reorg (`reorg`) — build first

*A crossroads with a plant in one corner. The plant is not always in the same corner. Mark your own door.*

- **First ten seconds.** A square room, the same from every side, with a fat column in the middle and an identical doorway in each wall. One landmark: a yucca in a pot beside one door. Walk round the column and the plant is beside a different door, its leaves still swinging, a drag line in the carpet pile. The door you were sure you came in by is not the one you came in by.
- **The aha.** The only landmark in the room belongs to the room. Put down one that is yours. A chalk mark, or anything from a pocket, is never moved.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** How many doorways, which corner the plant goes to next (a shuffled order, not a rotation), and what the landmark is (plant, cooler, coat stand, wet-floor sign). Two parts down the place forges your mark on every door, and its copies are all the same copy.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: you leave by the wrong door and walk a loop. Solving: once something of yours is on the floor, the plant goes back to its first corner and stays there, and where it last stood there is a battery pack it had been standing on.
- **Verbs.** Marking with chalk. Dropping. Looking away (the column does it for you). Walking.
- **Why it is not busywork.** There is nothing to operate. You notice you are being lied to, and one gesture ends it for good.
- **What it needs.** The room must be able to ask whether a chalk mark or a dropped thing is inside it (inventory-world.js already reports both; I need that readable from a room). About 9 x 9 m, 6,000 triangles, 6 meshes. Teaches: mark. It also gives chalk its first real use.
- **Stands on.** 12 (Simpler puzzle, stronger epiphany); 5 (The best thing in the room must matter)

### 4. Out of Hours (`out-of-hours`)

*The lights are on a sensor. Stand still until they forget you, and the room does what it does when it thinks nobody is there.*

- **First ten seconds.** A store room of tall steel cabinets, all shut. By the door, a long handover note in somebody's handwriting, the kind you stop to read. While you read, the tubes click off one by one. In the dark every cabinet swings open with a small light inside it, like a fridge. Most are bare shelves. Take a step and the tubes ping on and every door slams.
- **The aha.** Stand where you want to be, and then wait. Turning your head is not moving. Pressing E is not moving.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** Which cabinets hold something, how many there are, how long the sensor takes (four to six seconds). You find out which by looking round from wherever you stood still, then go and stand at that one. Two parts down the stand-ins from Blackout Bay are in the room: the dark you make is theirs, a torch holds one of them, and you have one torch.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: five seconds a try. Solving: one or two battery packs. One cabinet holds a standee in a lanyard, which costs nothing but the jump.
- **Verbs.** Standing still. Light and dark. Looking. Listening (the clicks), backed by the tubes themselves.
- **Why it is not busywork.** The answer is to do nothing, on purpose, in the dark, down here.
- **What it needs.** world.setLights already dims a room live (the night porter uses it); a room needs to be allowed to call it. Cabinet doors as moving parts. About 8 x 8 m, 10,000 triangles, 18 meshes, 8 lights. Teaches: still.
- **Stands on.** 12 (Simpler puzzle, stronger epiphany); 11 (Introduce, develop, twist, conclude)

### 5. The Draught (`draught`)

*Everything hanging in this room leans the same way. Lean on the wall it is leaning toward.*

- **First ten seconds.** Rows of lanyards on hooks, a string of leaving-do bunting, a blind with half its slats. All of it stirs, and all of it is pulled toward one stretch of blank wall, the nearest things most. A thin whistle gets louder there. No tape on that wall, no marks. Lean on it and it gives.
- **The aha.** The tape on walls is other visitors guessing. The air is the building's own tell. You can find a thin wall that nobody has marked.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** Which wall and where along it, what is hanging, how strong the pull. Two parts down nothing hangs in the room, and you bring the instrument: a balloon on its string, or a dropped sheet of paper that slides. At the last wall of the visit, of all the taped doors, only the real one draws.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: the wall knocks back, two seconds. Solving: an earlier visitor's cupboard behind it with two battery packs.
- **Verbs.** Leaning on a wall. Looking. Listening, backed by what you can see. Carrying, when it comes back.
- **Why it is not busywork.** It turns the one verb you arrived with into something you can aim.
- **What it needs.** The cupboard is inside the room's own footprint behind a false wall, and thin.js's pure lean rule can be reused, so the first room needs nothing. The return at the last wall needs the builder's session: the taped doors there are theirs. About 9 x 8 m, 9,000 triangles, 10 meshes. Needs: lean. Teaches: draught.
- **Stands on.** 6 (Clue everything; the link must make sense afterwards); 14 (Gate by knowledge, so the rule is the reward)

### 6. Desire Line (`desire-line`)

*The signs point everywhere. The carpet is worn toward one door, and toward one bit of wall.*

- **First ten seconds.** A junction with the same printed sign hung over every doorway, WAY OUT, including the one you came in by. In the carpet a pale trodden track runs from your door and forks. One branch goes to a doorway. A fainter one goes to a blank wall and stops at it, with scuffs on the skirting and hand marks on the paper at shoulder height.
- **The aha.** The signs are the place's. The wear is people's. From here on you read floors.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** The worn doorway is the one that really is nearer the way down on this floor, worked out from the plan, so it is true and different every time. Where the wall branch ends is rolled. Two parts down the place prints wear of its own: the same footprint at exact intervals, straight as a ruler.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: a detour, or a wall that knocks back. Solving: the right direction, and behind the wall a snack and a piece of chalk.
- **Verbs.** Looking. Walking. Leaning on a wall.
- **Why it is not busywork.** It is evidence, and reading it pays in every room after this one.
- **What it needs.** Nothing new: roomDistances in gen.js says which door is nearer the way down, and wear is floor decals. About 8 x 8 m, 4,000 triangles, 5 meshes. Needs: lean. Teaches: worn. It is the cheapest room on the list.
- **Stands on.** 1 (Ask why it is here); 4 (No planted red herrings)

### 7. Retention (`retention`)

*Box files by year. Your tape says it is 1996. Anything filed after that has not happened yet.*

- **First ten seconds.** One shelf of six to eight box files, a year in big figures on each spine with a project's name. Some spines are thumbed soft; some are crisp. Open a box from a year at or before the one on your picture: whatever somebody filed. Open one from after: nothing in it, the picture tears, and the stamp in the corner now reads that year. You have spent the time getting there.
- **The aha.** What is dated after your tape is the place's, and it costs you to look.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** The stamp's year, the years on the spines, and what is in the real ones. Each part of the building is already two to five years earlier than the one above, so the same shelf gets meaner on the way down with no extra rule. Two parts down it is months within one year, and the spines are all crisp.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: twenty seconds of battery a box. Solving: one or two battery packs and a snack, at no cost, in ten seconds.
- **Verbs.** The date stamp. Static on the tape. The battery.
- **Why it is not busywork.** Every box is a bet, and you can make it with knowledge instead of luck.
- **What it needs.** The stamp readable from a room (as Room Booking) and settable (hud.setStamp exists). env.camera.drain exists. About 7 x 6 m, 8,000 triangles, 8 meshes. Needs: stamp. Archive floors.
- **Stands on.** 13 (Never trial and error); 3 (Know you are right before you try)

### 8. Presenteeism (`presenteeism`)

*A desk with your name on it. While you are at it, the stock cupboard across the room is open. So leave your jacket on the chair, as it were.*

- **First ten seconds.** Hot desks with name plates: your colleagues' real names, and yours. Stand at yours and the monitor wakes (WELCOME BACK), something clunks, and a shutter across the room rolls up. Step away and it rolls down faster than you can get there. At anybody else's desk the screen shows their task and nothing happens.
- **The aha.** The desk does not know you. It knows something is on the chair.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** Which desk is yours, where the cupboard is, what is in it. A stack of paper is always on your desk, so the room can be solved with empty pockets. Two parts down something tidies away what you left while you are not looking at it, and you cross the room backwards.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: time. Solving: two battery packs, and you may go back for what you left or not.
- **Verbs.** Carrying and putting down. Your name and the office's. Walking backwards, when it comes back.
- **Why it is not busywork.** Plainly: a thing on a switch is one of the oldest moves there is. What is ours is that the thing is whatever you can bear to leave, and that the joke is true.
- **What it needs.** The same read of what has been put down in the room as Reorg. Desks, chairs and monitors are the office's own props, which are heavy: about 10 x 9 m, 30,000 triangles, 10 meshes. Teaches: standin.
- **Stands on.** 1 (Ask why it is here); 10 (One idea at a time, and watch people play)

### 9. Previously (`previously`)

*The picture says PLAY. Somebody walks in, goes straight to the third cabinet, bottom drawer. Then it says REC again and the drawer is shut.*

- **First ten seconds.** You step in and the red dot goes out. PLAY, the counter on some other number, tracking lines. A grainy figure comes in by your door, crosses to one of five identical drawer units, kneels, fills its pockets and leaves by another door. The picture tears. REC. Every drawer is shut.
- **The aha.** That was not something hunting you. It was somebody who was here before, and the tape kept it. They showed you the drawer, and the door they went on by.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** Which drawer, which door they leave by (the one nearer the way down), and whose shape it is. Two parts down the tape shows somebody doing the thing that got them, which is the one not to do. And what the place plays back loops, with a jump in it.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: nothing but the seconds spent on empty drawers. Solving: the pack they could not carry, and a direction.
- **Verbs.** The camcorder: the tape, REC and PLAY, static. Looking. Standing.
- **Why it is not busywork.** Plainly: this is the lightest puzzle here. The work is realising what you are watching. I would keep it as an event, and because the harder rooms can then speak through it.
- **What it needs.** From the lead: a way to put the picture into PLAY and back (hud.js has recording(); it needs its opposite). A figure that walks (cast/walk.js and the avatar kit exist). About 9 x 8 m, 10,000 triangles, 9 meshes. Teaches: tape.
- **Stands on.** 9 (The clock and the hints belong to the world); 6 (Clue everything; the link must make sense afterwards)

### 10. The Copy Room (`copy-room`)

*Five hand-drawn arrows on five cupboards, the same down to the smudge. One is not.*

- **First ten seconds.** A photocopier in the middle with its lid up and a drawing on the glass. Five shallow cupboards, each with an arrow and HERE in marker beside it. They look like somebody's. Then you see that four are the same drawing: the same wobble, the same drip, on taped-up paper. The fifth is drawn on the wall itself. Close to, the four crawl on your picture the way print does on video.
- **The aha.** The place can copy a hand. It cannot make two different things.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** The drawing itself is drawn from the seed, as are how many cupboards there are and which arrow is the original. Two parts down the same rule sorts out forged chalk in Reorg and printed footprints after Desire Line.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: a cupboard of blank copies slumps out on you, five seconds, and you are left holding paper (which the lady with the shredder will take). Solving: the stash behind the real one.
- **Verbs.** Looking closely. The camcorder's picture.
- **Why it is not busywork.** Plainly: it is a spot-the-difference. It is kept to one difference of kind among at most six, and its point is that it changes how you read rooms you have already walked through.
- **What it needs.** A stroke drawn by the seed into a texture (kit.tex.custom does it). A voxel copier. About 9 x 8 m, 12,000 triangles, 9 meshes. Needs: worn.
- **Stands on.** 12 (Simpler puzzle, stronger epiphany); 6 (Clue everything; the link must make sense afterwards)

### 11. Keep Your Head Down (`head-down`)

*Chest-high partitions and a team lead who looks over them. Below the line, nobody is there.*

- **First ten seconds.** A cubicle farm, partitions to your chest. At the far side a figure on a low platform turns slowly, and as its look crosses the room the monitor in each cubicle wakes and sleeps again, a wave you can see coming. If the wave passes you with your head above the partitions you are walked to its desk for a word.
- **The aha.** The saying is literal. Under the line you are not in the room. Or you move only while the wave is somewhere else: both work, so crouching is never required.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** The partitions, how fast and which way the look sweeps, where the lead sits, which cubicle has the drawer worth reaching. Two parts down it stacks with whatever hunts by sight.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: seven seconds of HOW ARE WE GETTING ON WITH (a real task), and you are put back at the door. Solving: across unseen, and a battery pack from a drawer under his nose.
- **Verbs.** Crouching. Walking. Looking. The office's real tasks.
- **Why it is not busywork.** It is hiding from your manager behind the furniture. It is nerve more than riddle, and the list wants one room like that.
- **What it needs.** The stack with the mascot needs line of sight that knows about height, which is the cast session's: ask before promising it. The first room needs nothing. About 12 x 10 m, 14,000 triangles, 20 meshes. Teaches: low.
- **Stands on.** 8 (Answer every attempt); 11 (Introduce, develop, twist, conclude)

### 12. Break Glass (`break-glass`)

*A fire alarm point. Hit it and for twenty seconds every held-open door on this part swings shut and every locked one lets go. And everything hears.*

- **First ten seconds.** A short room. A red box at hand height with a worn patch round it. A fire door held open on a magnet. A heavy door with a lock box and no handle. A bell. Press: bell and strobes, the held door swings to, the heavy one clicks open on a cupboard.
- **The aha.** Doors on this part of the building obey the alarm. You can shut a door you are not standing at, or call the thing that follows you to here while you go there.
- **What the seed changes, and how it comes back.** Which doorways on the floor have fire doors is already the seed's. So is where this room lies and where the hunter is when you press. The answer is a judgement about this floor on this visit.
- **Failing and solving.** Failing: you have told it where you are. Solving: the cupboard's packs, and a floor full of shut doors between you and it.
- **Verbs.** Shutting a door. Listening, backed by strobes. Walking.
- **Why it is not busywork.** Plainly: it is a tool with a price more than a puzzle. I would build it last, and only if the cast session wants it.
- **What it needs.** The most outside help of any: env.doors to shut every pair (the porter does this), and a way to tell the hunter where the bell is, which is the cast session's. About 6 x 9 m, 5,000 triangles, 8 meshes. Needs: doors. Teaches: alarm.
- **Stands on.** 8 (Answer every attempt); 11 (Introduce, develop, twist, conclude)

## The three I would build first

Now Serving, Room Booking, Reorg.

- Three different verbs, so the first three do not feel like one idea three times: not looking, the camcorder's own stamp, and something of yours on the floor.
- Each makes something the level already has mean more. Now Serving is the third beat after the standees and the hallway. Room Booking makes the date stamp, which is on screen for the whole visit, into an instrument. Reorg is the first reason to carry chalk.
- Each fails in a different currency: your place, eight seconds in a meeting, a walk round the block. None costs a heart.
- They ask the least of other sessions: one read-only value (the time on the stamp) and one read of what is lying in a room.
- Two of them open a second room at no new cost in teaching: Room Booking pays for Retention, Reorg for The Copy Room.

Fourth would be Out of Hours, which has the strongest single moment on the list and depends on a room being allowed to dim itself.

## Three I cut

- **The Clock Wall.** A wall of world clocks, one of which agrees with the stamp on your picture. It is the same aha as Room Booking, but all it asks is that you match a number to a number: a code on a note where the note is the screen. Room Booking keeps the aha and gives it a consequence.
- **Prove You Are Not A Robot.** A wall of nine framed photographs and a sign asking for every one with a stapler in it. It is funny to describe. It is a chore by construction, and that is the thing the brief rules out.
- **The Supergraphic.** Paint across pillars that only reads as an arrow from one spot on the floor. Finding the spot is a pixel hunt in three dimensions, and lining scenery up into a sign is too close to one well-known game's signature. It would be recognised.
